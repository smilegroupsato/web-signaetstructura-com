Who Benefited from the Privilege to Issue Banknotes?

Austria, 1816–1878: private note-issuing privilege, concessional credit to the government, wartime convertibility, and the division of bank profits.

Research as of 2026-11-08Published

“The power to print money makes its issuer rich.” The story is more complicated. In nineteenth-century Austria, shareholders could benefit from issuing banknotes, while the treasury obtained preferential loans and, by 1878, an explicit share of bank profits. The right to issue money was not identical to the right to retain its benefits.

A privately financed note issuer gained a public privilege

The Austrian Nationalbank was established in 1816 and received an exclusive note-issuing privilege on 15 July 1817. Despite its name, it was initially financed by private shareholders. The authorities established a monetary institution by vesting the capacity to issue notes in a privately funded bank. [1] [3]

The bank financed commercial business, but the state was also its borrower. The bank's official history lists government advances of 20 million florins in 1820, 30 million in 1821, and 12 million in 1831. Its silver and coin reserve ratio fell from 74.6% in 1818 to 10.3% in 1831. These are amounts associated with different years and balance-sheet measures: they must not be added together as if they were a single outstanding debt, nor do the reserve ratios alone measure a social loss. [1]

Privileges, subsidised credit and the two directions of advantage

The economist G. F. Knapp argued that when the state accepts privately issued banknotes for payments to the public treasury, it broadens their circulation and can improve the profitability of the issuing bank and the position of its shareholders. This is a claim about the mechanism of public acceptance, not a calculation of a particular bank's realised profit. [4]

The other side was less favourable to the bank's owners. A 2016 study published by the Oesterreichische Nationalbank estimates that, between 1816 and 1847, concessional advances to the government carried interest rates averaging two to three percentage points below the market yield on government bonds. The state's gain therefore included an implicit saving on borrowing costs, even before explicit government profit-sharing rules were adopted. [3]

War changed loans, convertibility and the rules of payment

A banking history published in 1911 records a government borrowing of 133 million florins during the 1859 war, as well as a separate 20 million in coin. The Bank Act of 1862, entering into operation in 1863, sought to restore constraints on note issue and regulate the government's outstanding obligations. [2]

In the war of 1866, however, the authorities altered note status, suspended convertibility and provided for temporary finance from the bank. Legislation dated 7 July 1866 authorised a temporary advance of up to 60 million florins. The 1911 account states that this sum was repaid in 1867. The legal ceiling should not be confused with a complete reconstruction of daily cash disbursements. [1] [2]

In 1867 the bank's dividend fell short of seven per cent; the bank invoked a contractual deficiency guarantee capped at one million florins. The sum, if any, actually paid by the government has not been independently verified in this review. [2]

In 1878, distribution of profits became a formal rule

The reorganisation into the Austro-Hungarian Bank made the division of distributable profit explicit. The priority order mattered: an initial shareholder dividend of up to five per cent of paid-in capital, a reserve allocation, additional shareholder payments until dividends reached seven per cent, and only then a division of remaining profits equally between shareholders and the two governments. The government share was divided seventy per cent to Austria and thirty per cent to Hungary, with the proceeds earmarked for reducing an old 80-million-florin state debt on which no interest was charged in this arrangement. [2] [3]

It would be wrong to say that the governments received half of all profits in every year. The fifty-fifty split applied only after the preceding allocations.

The 2016 historical reconstruction also counts benefits that do not appear as ordinary dividends, including the government's interest savings on favourable loans. It reports a point in 1846 at which private shareholders received more than sixty per cent of the study's definition of effective distributed profit. That figure is NOT a share of total social wealth; it is a result under the researchers' defined measure of financial benefits. [3]

What the sources establish—and what remains open

The evidence shows that the right to issue notes and the ultimate destination of the benefits did not necessarily coincide. The state could confer an issuing privilege, obtain cheap credit and eventually claim an explicit share of profits; shareholders could collect dividends, and war could rewrite the terms of note convertibility.

But the larger distributional question remains open. Without consistent annual bank accounts, shareholder registers, taxes, wages, prices and silver premia, the gain or loss borne by workers, taxpayers and banknote holders cannot be established. Knapp's theoretical sequence is a useful guide, not proof that every Austrian episode followed his account.
